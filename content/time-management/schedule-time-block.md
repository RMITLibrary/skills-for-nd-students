                <p>If planning your study time has ever felt overwhelming, complicated, or exhausting, you're not doing it wrong. You may just need a strategy that works better for you. So let's walk through how to create a weekly planner that works with your brain, not against it. We'll demonstrate this using Outlook Calendar, but the same ideas can be used in any digital calendar.</p>
				<p class="h4">Step one: fixed commitments</p>
				<p>Start by adding your fixed commitments. Anything with a set time such as classes, work shifts, appointments or caring responsibilities. In outlook into these first and set them to repeat weekly where possible. This reduces mental load and prevents you from having to replan the same things every week.</p>
				<p class="h4"> Step two: flexible study time</p>
				<p>Next, block out time for flexible activities.</p>
				<p>Starting with your individual study time. This might include unsigned readings, assignment work, or reviewing class content at university. This step is especially important for a full time student. The average recommendation is around 10 to 20 hours per week per subject, but your commitment may be more or less depending on the course, so it's worth double checking what to recommended for each of your subjects.</p>
				<p class="strong">Be as specific as possible.</p>
				<p>Instead of labeling a block study, try reading or assignment drafting. Aim for manageable chunks of about 1 to 2 hours, because smaller, clearly defined tasks can make starting feel more achievable.</p>
				<p class="h4">Step three: buffer time</p>
				Now let's add buffer time. Many plans fail because they assume perfect focus and zero delays, which just isn't realistic.</p>
				<p>Between tasks and short buffers around 10 to 15 minutes to allow transitions or things running over. In outlook, you can block these as their own events. Think of buffers as protective space, not wasted time.</p>
				<p class="h4">Step four: breaks and wellbeing</p>
				<p>An essential step is scheduling breaks and wellbeing time. Without regular rest, it becomes much harder to focus, regulate emotions and stay motivated.</p>
				<p>Schedule breaks, meals, downtime and recovery just like you would a class, seeing them in your calendar can serve as a reminder and permission to actually take them.</p>
				<p class="h4">Step five: color coding</p>
				<p>The next step is to apply color coding, as this helps visually to organize your week. Choose a small number of colors and use them consistently. For example, one color for classes or study, one for work, one for personal or wellbeing time.</p>
				<p>The goal is to understand your week at a glance without having to think too hard about it.</p>
				<p class="h4">Step six: reminders</p>
				<p>Next, set up reminders that support you, not stress you out. Outlook lets you choose when reminders appear. So try 10 or 15 minutes before events or earlier for tasks that need more preparation. If notifications become annoying or distracting.</p>
				<p>Adjust them to prioritize the ones you really need.</p>
				<p class="h4">Step seven.</p>
				<p>Weekly reset. Finally, build in a weekly reset, ideally on Fridays. The idea is to take five minutes to review what's coming up and make changes as needed. Remember that a good planner is flexible. If you'd like more personalized support, you're always welcome to speak with an academic skills advisor about setting up a weekly planner that works best for you.</p>
